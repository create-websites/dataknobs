Predictive Monitoring for Stream Gages

Using AI/ML to Estimate Waterflow When Sensors Fail

Saving Lives and Reducing Infrastructure Costs

The Emergency Response Challenge

Emergency response teams require precise, up-to-date waterflow information to identify flood-prone areas during storms and floods. Yet, the current system for gathering this vital data is hindered by substantial operational and financial obstacles.

The Current Infrastructure Problem

💰 Massive Infrastructure Investment

The US government has allocated $20 billion to deploy stream gages nationwide. Each gage has an estimated cost of $50,000, with an annual maintenance expense of $200 million to uphold the network.

⚠️ Frequent Sensor Failures

Gauges often malfunction, particularly in times of flooding and storms when they are most crucial. The sensors are frequently damaged or destroyed by floating trees and debris in the river.

🚨 Data When It Matters Most

During floods and storms, emergency response teams require immediate access to waterflow data to identify at-risk areas. However, gages are prone to failure or damage during these critical situations.

❌ Why Traditional Approaches Fail

Gages are not suitable for predictive maintenance as they can fail unexpectedly in extreme conditions. Predicting waterflow accurately is challenging due to the intricate relationships with weather, snow, temperature, and dam operations.

The Core Insight

The issue lies in not being able to predict the failure of gages or forecast waterflow on their own. Instead, it's about staying aware of waterflow even when sensors malfunction. The answer is to employ machine learning to predict waterflow at malfunctioning gages using data from nearby, functioning gages.

The ML Solution: Waterflow Estimation

Instead of attempting to forecast gage failures or exact waterflow levels, we utilize machine learning to capitalize on connections between neighboring gages. In the event of a gage failure, we can reliably estimate its waterflow by analyzing data from correlated gages within the same geographical area.

The ML Approach

Traditional Stream Flow Monitoring

  • Stream gage collects waterflow data every 15 minutes
  • Data made available to government agencies in real-time
  • But gages break frequently, especially during floods and storms
  • Critical data becomes unavailable at the worst possible time

ML-Powered Waterflow Estimation

  • Create clusters of gages with similar waterflow patterns
  • Identify correlations between gages within clusters
  • If a gage malfunctions, utilize cluster correlations to approximate its waterflow.
  • Achieve >90% accuracy::emergency teams get reliable data when needed most

Key Advantages

✓ Unprecedented Accuracy

Government agencies can acquire accurate waterflow information exceeding 90% reliability even in cases of gage failure, ensuring dependable data for making emergency response decisions.

✓ Significant Cost Savings

Organizations can cut costs significantly by maintaining system visibility even when sensor availability is not perfect, saving money on emergency repairs and operational expenses.

✓ Safer Emergency Response

Emergency responders are able to make informed decisions regarding flood risk even in the absence of functioning sensors. In times of peril, no team should have to endanger lives by replacing faulty gauges.

✓ Practical Deployment

The solution is not dependent on replacing or perfect maintenance of all gages. It can work with the existing gages by using intelligent estimation to reduce the risk of failures.

Real-World Impact

In regions prone to flooding like Alaska, where gages often malfunction in harsh winter and storm conditions, teams can now avoid risking lives by relying on an ML system that accurately estimates waterflow. This enables emergency response decisions to be made even when gages are not functioning.

Understanding Data Patterns for ML Success

The strategy for creating a successful ML model is highly influenced by the patterns present in your data. Simple models are suitable for clear patterns, while more complex approaches are needed for subtle patterns.

Three Pattern Complexity Levels

1
Obvious Patterns

Data Characteristics: Clear, distinct patterns are visible

Requirements: Need less data, fewer features, simple models

Expected Outcome: Highly accurate results with straightforward approaches

Example: Strong seasonal waterflow patterns with clear peaks and valleys

2
Less Obvious Patterns

Data Characteristics: Patterns exist but are subtle or multi-layered

Requirements: Need more historical data, additional features, complex models

Expected Outcome: Good accuracy with sophisticated modeling approaches

Example: Waterflow influenced by multiple correlated factors (weather, temperature, reservoir levels)

3
Even Less Obvious Patterns

Data Characteristics: Patterns are highly complex or non-linear

Requirements: Different algorithms, many features, complex or deep learning models

Expected Outcome: Moderate accuracy::inherent complexity limits predictability

Example: Extreme events, unusual weather combinations with unprecedented impacts

Key Data Exploration Insight

The complexity of your patterns determines your approach. Begin by exploring your data to comprehend pattern complexity before resorting to deep learning or intricate models. Simple, interpretable models can often uncover obvious patterns, while more complex patterns may necessitate advanced techniques that have limitations in predictability. It is crucial to familiarize yourself with your data before selecting a model.

ML Model Architecture for Waterflow Prediction

The ML model is structured into four key phases: data collection with correlation analysis, training of machine learning models, management of flooding events, and real-time forecasting. This methodical process guarantees precision and dependability in crucial scenarios.

Four-Phase Model Architecture

1
Phase 1: Correlation Analysis & Clustering

Compute once per year: Examine stream data from NWIS to form clusters of gages according to time series resemblance and pinpoint gages displaying comparable surges or declines in waterflow. Spotlight regions lacking clustering (potential coverage gaps).

Output: Gage clusters with strong correlations ready for modeling

2
Phase 2: ML Training with SHEM

Compute once per year: Develop machine learning models for all stream gauges utilizing SHEM (Streamgage Hydrologic Estimate Using Machine Learning). Distinguish between easily predictable (high correlations) and challenging to predict (low correlations, remote locations) gauges. Create reliability models based on past data and validate them across various datasets.

Output: Trained models with confidence scores for each gage

3
Phase 3: Dynamic Model Application

Real-time execution: In the event of a stream gage malfunction, locate operational gages within the same grouping and potentially update the model in real-time with new data. This guarantees that forecasts adjust to present circumstances instead of solely depending on past trends.

Output: Adaptive models responsive to current conditions

4
Phase 4: Real-Time Prediction & Insights

On-demand execution: Forecast waterflow for malfunctioning gauges by utilizing operational gauges within the group. Obtain critical insights on the necessity of developing new models or features to enhance efficiency and precision. Enhance the system continuously by evaluating prediction outcomes.

Output: Real-time waterflow estimates for emergency response teams

Model Architecture Advantages
  • Correlation-Based: Utilizes geographical and hydrological relationships instead of attempting to forecast exact water flow.
  • Clustering: Groups similar gages for more accurate predictions within homogeneous regions
  • Adaptive: Retrains dynamically when needed to account for changing conditions
  • Scalable: One model per gage, enabling parallel training and prediction
  • Transparent: Identifies easy vs. difficult to predict gages, guiding improvement efforts

Model Results & Performance

The ML model displays different levels of accuracy for different gages, revealing distinct patterns that shed light on why certain locations are more predictable than others. Recognizing these patterns helps in refining the model further.

Three Categories of Results

Category 1: Accurate Predictions

Performance: Excellent correlation between predicted and actual waterflow

Explanation: Strong geographic/hydrologic correlations with nearby gages enable accurate estimation

Characteristics: Gages with multiple similar neighbors in the cluster

Use Case: Safe to use for emergency response decisions

Category 2: Good with Limitations

Performance: Accurate predictions for gradual changes, struggles with sharp declines

Explanation: Models capture general trends but miss rapid state changes

Characteristics: Normal conditions well-modeled, extreme events less accurate

Use Case: Use with caution during extreme weather; supplement with additional features

Category 3: Needs Improvement

Performance: Moderate accuracy overall

Explanation: Insufficient training data, poor correlations with nearby gages, or unusual local conditions

Improvement Path: Candidate for deep learning approaches or additional feature engineering

Action Items: Gather additional historical data and identify more predictive features such as weather, elevation, and vegetation.

Key Insights from Results

What the Results Tell Us
  • Clustering Works: Gages with strong geographic/hydrologic correlations yield accurate predictions
  • Pattern Complexity Varies: Certain areas display clear patterns that can be easily modeled, while others exhibit intricate patterns that require more advanced approaches.
  • Extreme Events Challenge: Models perform adequately under normal conditions but struggle when faced with sudden and drastic changes.
  • Geographic Factors Matter: Isolated gages or unique hydrologic conditions reduce predictability
  • Feature Engineering Opportunity: Incorporating external data such as weather, temperature, and reservoir levels could enhance forecasts for challenging gauges.
Practical Impact on Emergency Response

Even with moderate accuracy on some gages, the system provides decision support for emergency teams. >90% accuracy on easily predictable gages covers many critical locations. For difficult-to-predict locations, the system flags risk levels and uncertainty, enabling teams to take appropriate precautions. Combined with manual inspection where possible, the ML estimates significantly improve situational awareness during floods and storms.

Implementation Framework for Predictive Monitoring

Developing a predictive monitoring system necessitates a methodical approach that merges technical advancements, operational coordination, and ongoing enhancements.

Implementation Phases

1
Data Exploration & Pattern Analysis

Gain insight from your data by examining historical waterflow patterns at each gage, identifying clusters of gages with similar patterns, and evaluating pattern complexity to determine the suitability of simple or complex models.

2
Clustering & Correlation Analysis

Utilize time series similarity metrics to detect groups of gages. Calculate correlations between gages within each group. Identify areas with subpar clustering. Document gages that are simple to forecast versus those that are more challenging.

3
Model Selection & Training

Begin with basic regression or time series models for easily predictable data points. Use more advanced models only when necessary due to data complexity. Update models annually using complete historical data and evaluate performance with holdout data to gauge real-world effectiveness.

4
Operational Integration

Utilize emergency response systems to integrate real-time waterflow estimates in the event of gage failures. Display confidence scores to assist teams in assessing reliability and set up alerts for critical gage unavailability. Offer training for emergency response teams on utilizing these estimates effectively.

5
Continuous Improvement

Track the accuracy of predictions in real-world settings, systematically pinpoint mispredicted instances, gather extra features to enhance accuracy, continuously improve models, and extend to challenging locations by collecting targeted data and refining features.

Success Factors

Keys to Implementation Success

Start with Data Understanding: Don't assume you need complex models. Explore your data first.

Leverage Correlations: Geographic and hydrologic correlations are more predictable than absolute values.

Identify Easy vs Hard: Segment gages by predictability. Use simpler approaches where they work.

Build Iteratively: Begin by addressing 80% of locations, then enhance to 90%, before finally addressing the more challenging remaining cases.

Integrate Operationally: The top model holds worth solely when utilized by emergency responders.

Making Predictive Monitoring Work for Your Organization

Predictive monitoring demonstrates how ML solves real operational problems. The key is not predicting the future with absolute precision, but rather staying aware of the situation despite sensor imperfections. By using relationships between adjacent sensors, you can accurately guess missing information.

The key to success is pragmatism. Begin by exploring your data to identify patterns, both obvious and subtle. Start by building simple models and only use complex approaches when necessary. Make sure to integrate these predictions with operations for practical application.

The value is substantial and multifaceted. Cut costs by preventing last-minute repairs. Enhance safety by avoiding risky sensor replacements. Improve emergency response by providing crucial data when it counts. Most importantly, protect lives by equipping decision-makers with essential information in critical situations.

Predictive monitoring isn't theoretical::it's practical, deployable, and impactful today. If you're overseeing water systems, power grids, infrastructure networks, or any distributed monitoring system, these principles remain relevant. The strategy remains consistent: utilize machine learning to predict missing sensor data using related neighboring sensors, stay informed even in the event of failures, and provide value when it is most crucial.
