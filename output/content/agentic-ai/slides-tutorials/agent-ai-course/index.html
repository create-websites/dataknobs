What you will learn

This introduction links agent capabilities with practical designs. Examine reactive and deliberative behaviors, utility and learning, then observe how tool use, planning, reflection, and collaboration enable real-world tasks.

The course also addresses adoption: identifying where agents can assist, understanding the constraints on their reliability, and assessing benefits without presuming that autonomy inherently enhances outcomes.

Key takeaway: An agent requires a defined goal, relevant observations, allowed actions, and a method to assess results. Learning and cooperation among multiple agents are optional design elements, not essential for every task.

Agent AI: an introductory course

AI agents are systems designed to accomplish tasks or objectives with a certain level of autonomy. This presentation covers their functionalities, key categories, real-world uses, operational workflows, advantages, and obstacles to adoption.

Eight agent capabilities

The deck emphasizes autonomy, learning, goal orientation, environmental interaction, reactive and proactive behavior, multi-agent coordination, personalization, and communication. These represent potential capabilities; a single agent is not required to possess all eight.

SLIDE 03 / 9

Reactive, deliberative, hybrid, utility and learning agents

Reactive agents act based on observations, whereas deliberative agents plan to achieve goals. Hybrid models integrate reactive responses with planning; utility-based agents evaluate potential outcomes, and learning agents adjust through feedback. These classifications may intersect within a single system.

02 · Patterns, benefits and adoption

SLIDE 05 / 9

Four design patterns

Reflection evaluates a candidate outcome; tool use links a model to external capabilities; planning arranges sequential actions; and multi-agent collaboration allocates tasks. These patterns can be integrated into a single workflow instead of being viewed as separate alternatives.

Back to slide index ↑
SLIDE 06 / 9

Challenges and adoption constraints

The deck highlights development cost, complexity, security, bias, transparency, data requirements, privacy, accountability, compliance, and narrow specialization. An effective implementation clearly defines responsibilities and action boundaries, then assesses performance on the specific task.

Back to slide index ↑
SLIDE 07 / 9

Potential benefits and how to assess them

Automation has the potential to lessen repetitive tasks, boost capacity, and enable quicker, more customized responses. These advantages are possible outcomes, not assured results. Evaluate completion rate, quality, response time, cost per successful task, and human review effort relative to a baseline.

Back to slide index ↑
SLIDE 08 / 9

Designing an agentic workflow

Tool use involves gathering information or executing actions; planning organizes the sequence of tasks; reflection evaluates and adjusts outcomes; collaboration designates specific roles. Feedback must relate to observable task outcomes, as self-assessment by itself does not confirm accuracy.

Questions answered

What is an AI agent?

An AI agent carries out a task or objective by processing information and choosing actions, operating within the limits set by its design and authorization.

What types of agents does the course cover?

Reactive, deliberative, hybrid, utility-based, and learning agents are classifications that may intersect.

What are the four agentic design patterns?

Reflection, tool use, planning and multi-agent collaboration.

Do all AI agents learn automatically?

No. Learning is a potential ability, not a necessity for all agents. Numerous systems function using fixed models, prompts, and rules until they are intentionally modified.

How should teams evaluate agent benefits?

Evaluate task success, quality, latency, cost per completed task, and review effort in comparison to the current workflow.
