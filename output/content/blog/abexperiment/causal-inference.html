Detailed guide · causal inference

Does Smoking Cause Cancer?

The smoking–lung cancer case clearly illustrates how science can prove a causal relationship without exposing individuals to harm. It also serves as a practical model for contemporary data science: specify the counterfactual, consider alternative explanations, declare assumptions, measure sensitivity to unseen bias, and integrate evidence from methods prone to different types of errors.

01 · Counterfactuals

The causal question is a missing-data problem

Suppose X The question is whether an individual smokes. For every person, we can consider two possible outcomes: Y(1)whether that individual would develop cancer as a result of smoking, and Y(0)whether the same individual would develop cancer in their absence. The individual causal effect is Y(1) − Y(0); the population target is commonly the average treatment effect, E[Y(1) − Y(0)].

The fundamental problem

Each individual exhibits only one of those possible outcomes. After we observe the outcome when smoking, the non-smoking outcome for that individual remains counterfactual: and the reverse holds true as well. Causal inference is the field focused on estimating that unobserved counterfactual based on explicit assumptions.

A randomized trial typically produces comparable treated and untreated groups through assignment. However, intentionally having people smoke for decades would be unethical. Thus, the smoking issue became a challenge for causal inference based on observational data.

02 · Evidence trail

From suspicion to causal consensus

  1. 1950 : case-control evidence. Doll & Hill and Wynder & Graham reported strong links between smoking and lung cancer.
  2. 1951 onward : prospective cohort evidence. The British Doctors Study documented smoking habits prior to disease onset, reinforcing the temporal relationship.
  3. 1957–58 : Fisher’s challenge. R. A. Fisher suggested that a hereditary constitutional factor could be responsible for both the inclination to smoke and the risk of developing cancer.
  4. 1959 : Cornfield’s sensitivity argument. Cornfield and his team questioned how powerful such an unmeasured factor would need to be to account for the observed relationship.
  5. 1964 : U.S. Surgeon General. The evidence was deemed causally adequate for linking cigarette smoking to lung cancer.
  6. 1965 and beyond. Hill presented nine criteria for interpreting observational data, and subsequent long-term cohort studies along with later genetic findings further supported the causal narrative.
03 · Observational data

Three designs, three different blind spots

01

Cross-sectional

Exposure and outcome are assessed simultaneously. This approach can identify prevalence and correlations, but it frequently cannot determine the temporal sequence.

Smoking example: a survey of current smoking and current lung disease.

Main risk: temporality is ambiguous.

02

Case-control

Begin with individuals diagnosed with the disease and matched controls, then retrospectively examine their previous exposures.

Smoking example: lung-cancer patients compared with controls in the early 1950 studies.

Main risks: recall bias and selection bias. The odds ratio is typically used as the natural measure of association.

03

Cohort

Document exposure prior to disease onset, then track individuals over time. This clearly confirms the exposure occurred before the outcome.

Smoking example: the British Doctors Study.

Main risks: long follow-up, cost and residual confounding.

04 · Association measures

Risk ratio, odds ratio and risk difference answer different questions

Odds ratio (OR)9.07In the case of a rare disease, the odds ratio approximates the risk ratio, which clarifies why case-control studies are valuable.

Association measures characterize patterns in observed data but do not, on their own, determine causal effects.

05 · Rival explanations

“Association is not causation” serves as a diagnostic guideline, not merely a catchphrase.

Chance

Sampling variation can produce a false pattern. Using large samples and conducting repeated replications minimize this possibility.

Reverse causation

The outcome or initial disease development alters the exposure. In studies on smoking, “sick quitters” can complicate straightforward comparisons.

Confounding

A shared cause influences both smoking and cancer. Fisher’s genetic hypothesis serves as the classic example.

Selection bias

The way participants are selected for a study can produce a false association: for instance, selecting hospital controls whose hospitalization is linked to smoking.

Information bias

Exposure or outcome is assessed differently among groups. One possible explanation is retrospective recall of smoking.

Causation

The causal interpretation gains credibility as alternative explanations are tackled through independent study designs and diverse evidence sources.

06 · Hill’s viewpoints

Nine lenses for judging an observational causal claim

Strength

Large associations are harder: though not impossible: to explain away by bias.

Consistency

Consistent results across different countries, populations, and study methodologies lessen reliance on the peculiarities of any single study.

Specificity

Weak in this instance because smoking leads to various diseases; specificity is not necessary to establish causation.

Temporality

The exposure must occur before the outcome. This was especially evident in prospective cohort studies.

Biological gradient

Risk rises with greater intensity and duration of smoking.

Plausibility

Carcinogens and biological mechanisms support the causal interpretation.

Coherence

Disease trends ought to align with established knowledge about exposure patterns.

Experiment

Risk decreases following cessation, offering evidence akin to intervention effects even in the absence of randomized initiation.

Analogy

Other inhaled carcinogens provide supporting analogies.

Important: Hill characterized these as perspectives rather than a rigid checklist. The presentation highlights that temporality is the sole logically necessary condition.

07 · Confounding

Fisher’s challenge: could a hidden common cause explain everything?

A confounder must meet three criteria: it influences the outcome, is linked to the exposure, and is not a result caused by the exposure. Fisher suggested a genetic “constitution” that could impact both the likelihood of smoking and the risk of developing lung cancer.

Genotype U → Smoking X → Lung cancer Y    and    Genotype U → Lung cancer Y

The causal graph matters because the path X ← U → Y enters the exposure through an incoming arrow. This is a back-door pathIf it stays open, comparing smokers to non-smokers combines the causal impact of smoking with underlying differences resulting from U.

08 · Sensitivity analysis

How strong would hidden confounding have to be?

Cornfield’s inequality

The deck outlines the traditional reasoning: if smoking genuinely had no causal impact and a binary hidden variable U accounted for the observed risk ratio, then U would have to be considerably more common in smokers than in non-smokers.

P(U | smoker) ÷ P(U | non-smoker) ≥ RR

For an observed RR near 9, the hidden factor would require a highly disproportionate distribution. If 11% of non-smokers possessed it, then 99% of smokers would have to carry it; any prevalence above 11.1% among non-smokers would push the necessary smoker prevalence over 100%. Considering the much stronger associations found in heavy smokers, the hidden-confounder explanation is even less credible.

The E-value

A contemporary sensitivity summary specifies the smallest association strength an unmeasured confounder must have with both the exposure and the outcome to account for an observed risk ratio.

E = RR + √(RR × (RR − 1))
17.5E-value shown in the deck for RR = 9.
119.5E-value shown for RR = 60.
PurposeMeasure the strength of unmeasured confounding instead of assuming it is absent.
09 · Back-door adjustment

Close confounding paths without blocking the causal effect

Pearl’s back-door criterion offers a graphical method for selecting an adjustment set. A sufficient set Z blocks all paths from X to Y that start with an arrow pointing into X and include no descendants of X.

P(Y | do(X=x)) = Σz P(Y | X=x, Z=z) · P(Z=z)

After identifying an appropriate adjustment set, common estimation methods include stratification, multivariable regression, matching, propensity score techniques, and inverse probability weighting.

Confounder

Adjust. For instance, age influences smoking habits as well as cancer risk. Controlling for age can block a back-door path.

Mediator

Do not adjust when estimating the total effect. A mechanism like tar/DNA damage exists along the pathway from smoking to cancer; controlling for it eliminates a portion of the effect you aim to assess.

Collider

Do not condition. If both smoking and cancer influence hospital admission, limiting the analysis to hospitalized patients can create a false association (Berkson’s bias).

A DoWhy workflow

from dowhy import CausalModel

model = CausalModel(
    data=df,
    treatment="smoker",
    outcome="lung_cancer",
    common_causes=["age", "sex", "ses", "occupation"],
)
estimand = model.identify_effect()
estimate = model.estimate_effect(
    estimand,
    method_name="backdoor.propensity_score_weighting",
)
refute = model.refute_estimate(
    estimand, estimate,
    method_name="random_common_cause",
)

The crucial step is not making the library call. It is formulating defensible causal assumptions prior to estimation, followed by stress-testing to determine if the result is fragile.

10 · Instrumental variables

Estimate causal effects even when important confounders are unmeasured

An instrumental variable Z induces variation in smoking X, serving as a quasi-random source of exposure differences. For a causal interpretation, three key assumptions are essential:

  1. Relevance: Z truly alters X. This can be empirically tested in the initial stage.
  2. Exclusion: Z influences cancer Y solely via smoking X. This relationship is typically not directly verifiable and requires substantive justification.
  3. Independence: Z does not have common unblocked causes with Y; therefore, its variation can be considered effectively random concerning the factors determining the outcome.

Candidate instruments in the smoking problem

CandidateWhy it can move smokingMain validity threat
Cigarette taxes and pricesHigher prices reduce smoking participation or intensity.Areas with varying tax rates might also differ in health policies, income levels, pollution, or other factors contributing to cancer.
Genetic variant rs16969968The deck indicates that each risk allele in CHRNA5 alters the number of cigarettes smoked per day among smokers.Pleiotropy may breach exclusion if the variant has a direct impact on lung biology.
Smoke-free laws and campaignsPolicy changes can create abrupt exposure changes.Policy implementations might overlap with other health developments, and cancer results often involve significant delays.

Wald estimator and 2SLS

βIV = Cov(Z,Y) ÷ Cov(Z,X)

Two-stage least squares initially models exposure based on the instrument (and covariates), then connects the predicted exposure component to the outcome. The deck also emphasizes the well-known weak-instrument rule of thumb: a first-stage F-statistic greater than 10 is commonly used as a preliminary check, but it does not ensure validity.

What does IV estimate? Within the conventional monotonicity framework, the estimand typically represents a local average treatment effect (LATE): the impact on “compliers” whose smoking behavior is genuinely influenced by the instrument.
11 · Mendelian randomization

Genes become the randomizer

Mendelian randomization employs genetic variants linked to an exposure as instrumental variables. Since alleles are determined at conception instead of being selected later in life, they can occasionally offer variation that is less influenced by behavioral and socioeconomic confounding.

The presentation employs smoking-intensity variants like rs16969968/rs1051730 to demonstrate the concept and highlights the historical irony: Fisher suggested genes as a confounding factor, whereas modern causal inference utilizes particular genetic variants as tools to investigate the effect of smoking.

Strength

Genetic allocation occurs before disease onset and smoking behavior, aiding in establishing temporality and addressing certain types of confounding.

Pleiotropy

The exclusion restriction is violated if the variant influences cancer via a mechanism other than smoking.

Population structure

Ancestry may be linked to both genetic variants and outcomes, leading to confounding if not properly managed.

Triangulation is the deeper strategy. The conclusion about smoking was not based on a single flawless study. Instead, it relied on the agreement among various designs, each with its own limitations.

13 · ML practitioner takeaways

Five habits that separate prediction from causal reasoning

  1. Draw the causal graph before fitting the model. Determine what you think causes what, and ensure the assumptions are transparent.
  2. Do not confuse predictive accuracy with causal validity. A model can achieve perfect prediction by using variables that are downstream, colliders, or proxies for confounders.
  3. Quantify sensitivity to hidden confounding. Determine the magnitude of the omitted bias necessary to reverse the finding.
  4. Reach for quasi-experimental variation when direct adjustment is inadequate. Instruments, policy breaks, and similar designs can detect effects based on varying assumptions.
  5. Triangulate. Favor a collection of methods with diverse blind spots rather than relying on one advanced estimator whose assumptions all break down in the same way.

A product analytics example

The statement “Users who adopt feature F churn 30% less” does not yet demonstrate causality. Users with high intent might both use the feature and remain longer; eligibility criteria could act as a collider; product rollout policies might generate an instrument or quasi-experimental setting. The causal challenge is to identify these underlying mechanisms prior to estimating “the effect of F.”

FAQ

Common questions about causal inference

What is the fundamental problem of causal inference?

For each person, we observe only the result corresponding to the exposure they actually experienced. The other possible outcome is counterfactual, meaning that estimating causal effects necessitates assumptions or design methods that allow the unobserved potential outcome to be inferred.

Why is association not enough?

Associations can arise without the exposure causing the outcome due to chance, reverse causation, confounding, selection bias, and information bias.

What is a confounder?

A variable linked to the exposure, influences the outcome, and is not a result of the exposure. Controlling for an adequate set of confounders can block back-door paths.

Why should I avoid adjusting for a mediator?

If your objective is the total causal effect, a mediator represents a component of the pathway through which the treatment acts. Controlling for it eliminates a portion of that effect.

Why is conditioning on a collider dangerous?

A collider is an effect influenced by two variables. Conditioning on a collider can induce a statistical association between these causes, even if there is no causal link between them.

What does an E-value tell me?

It indicates the strength of association an unmeasured confounder must have with both the exposure and the outcome, measured on a risk-ratio scale, to account for an observed association.

When should I consider an instrumental variable?

When key confounders are unmeasurable but there exists a credible source of variation that alters the exposure, is not linked to the causes of the outcome, and influences the outcome solely via the exposure.

Does a high first-stage F-statistic prove an instrument is valid?

No. It pertains to instrument strength, rather than the exclusion or independence assumptions. An instrument can be strong yet invalid and still yield a biased causal estimate.
